## Statistical inputs

Every figure below is tagged by provenance. **[measured]** — published from a named
study with a stated sample. **[derived]** — arithmetic on a measured figure, shown.
**[speculation]** — engineering estimate with no published source; these are the
numbers to attack first, and the ones to re-check if a real data source appears.

**Two populations, differing by ~7x — this is the important finding.** The blend above
describes *all posts on the platform*. A second study measures *brand accounts*

| Population | Comments/post | Sample | Provenance |
|---|---|---|---|
| All posts, platform-wide | **~23** | 70M posts, Jan 2024–Dec 2025 | [measured] Socialinsider 2026 benchmark |

The mean therefore depends almost entirely on customer mix, and varies by roughly 7x
across plausible mixes. Respondology's population skews to large enterprise brands;
Blotato's self-serve, creator-and-marketer positioning sits closer to the platform-wide
figure, pushed upward by two effects:

- **Opt-in selection** [speculation]: §0 targets tier 2, per-post opt-in watching. A
  user subscribes a post they expect engagement on, so watched posts are not a random
  sample.
- **Customer-mix drift** [speculation]: accounts that pay for a scheduling tool engage
  more than the median account, and a B2B product moving upmarket shifts toward the
  Respondology population over time.

**Planning figures — three numbers doing three different jobs:**

| Number | Value | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Planning mean | **~50 comments/post** [speculation] | steady-state sizing: instances, storage, write rate, LLM cost |
| Enterprise-tenant mean | **~161** [derived] | the segment ceiling; what a large-brand tenant actually looks like |
| Per-post cap | **500** [policy] | pagination bound, single-fetch memory bound, stop-syncing threshold |

~50 is roughly 2x the platform-wide blend, covering both effects above without adopting
the enterprise figure wholesale. **500 is a per-post ceiling, never a fleet-wide
multiplier** — using it as a mean inflates every downstream figure by ~25x. Note the
product consequence of keeping it as a cap: a post exceeding 500 has comments the
customer cannot see. That is accepted, and stated.

### B. Reply-eligible share

**This is the weakest-sourced input in the plan and should be treated as such.** No
study was found that directly partitions comments into "warrants a substantive reply"
versus "does not." One measured anchor exists, and it bounds the problem from one side
only:

| Finding | Value | Provenance |
|---|---|---|
| Comments that are toxic, spam, or off-brand | **~20%** | [measured] Respondology, 118.4M comments across 450+ brands |
| Instagram comments/captions containing emoji | ~50% | [measured] but *containing*, not emoji-only — does not bound low-effort share |
| Comments that are emoji-only or single-word | no source found | [speculation] |
| Comments warranting a substantive reply | **20-30%** | [speculation] |

The measured 20% toxic/spam figure is useful because those comments should be
moderated or hidden rather than replied to, so it is a floor on the
*not-reply-eligible* share, not a component of the reply-eligible one.

| Account type | Substantive-reply share | Provenance |
|---|---|---|
| Discussion-driving, high-engagement | 35-40% | [speculation] |
| **Typical, mixed content — plan at 25%** | **20-30%** | [speculation] |
| Broadcast, meme, announcement | 10-15% | [speculation] |

Two framings matter more than the number:

- **Eligible share is not reply rate.** Actual replies sent are the minimum of what is
  eligible and what the platform write budget and cost ceiling permit (table C). A
  higher figure would be a policy aspiration rather than throughput — table C shows
  anything near 75% is unachievable on Instagram regardless of intent.
- **The low-signal filter is baseline, not a scaling lever.** It removes most volume
  before the expensive model call, and it does not need a frontier model to do it.
  This is the single highest-leverage decision in the reply pipeline.

### C. Platform API rate limits

> **Platform rate limiting is the main bottleneck in this system, and it deserves
> deeper research than anything else here.** Every other constraint turned out to have
> slack: Postgres sits two orders of magnitude below its wall, no service needs more
> than one instance for throughput, and LLM cost is a line item once the filter and
> prompt caching are applied. The platform quotas are the only place a design choice
> gets *rejected* rather than merely made expensive — flat polling wasn't costly, it was
> **disallowed**, at ~22x over what Meta permits. The figures below already carry the
> two most load-bearing speculations in the plan (the daily-active fraction and the
> share of the pool available to comments), and they swing utilization between ~20% and
> ~64%. Treat this section as the one most worth attacking with real telemetry.

All [measured] from platform documentation or vendor summaries, except where noted.
**These are the binding constraints on the whole design** — tighter than anything in
our own infrastructure, and the reason §1.4's throughput-target framing is the wrong
shape.

| Platform | Read limit | Write limit | Scope | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Instagram / Facebook** | 200 calls/hour/user, Graph API Platform limit | **60 writes/hour/user on `/media/comments`** | **app-wide pool** = 200 × daily active users, rolling 60 min | Business Use Case limits override where both apply: 4800 × 24h impressions, resets daily — so the real budget is dynamic, not a constant |
| **YouTube** | 10,000 quota units/day, `commentThreads.list` = 1 unit/page | — | **per project**, app-wide, does not scale with users | Hard ceiling for the entire application; resets midnight PT |
| **TikTok** | Display API 600 req/min/endpoint; Research API 1,000 req/day, 100 records/req | — | per endpoint | Research API caps at ~100,000 records/day |
| **X / Twitter** | Recent search 450 req/15min/app, 300/15min/user, 100 results/req | metered | per app and per user | Pay-per-use since Feb 2026: $0.005/post read, **capped 2M reads/month**; Basic $200/mo and Pro $5,000/mo closed to new signups; Enterprise from ~$42,000/mo |
| **LinkedIn** | **not published** | not published | application-level and member-level, 24h window, resets midnight UTC | Must be read from the Developer Portal per endpoint; email alert at 75% of quota. [speculation] until an app exists to query |

**Three consequences the plan does not currently account for:**

1. **Instagram's 60 writes/hour/account is the reply ceiling, and the old 75% reply
   rate sat on top of it.** At the previous 1,448 replies/sec across ~10^5 accounts
   that is ~52 writes/hour/account, or 87% of the hard cap with zero headroom and
   assuming perfectly even distribution. At the revised ~49/sec it is ~1.8/hour, under
   3%. The write limit independently confirms that 75% was never deliverable.
2. **YouTube is infeasible at this scale on default quota, at any poll interval.**
   Post arrival alone exceeds 10,000 units/day before any polling frequency is chosen.
   The remedy is a Google quota-increase audit or a push-based path, neither of which
   is an engineering lever.
3. **Meta's and YouTube's quotas are shared pools, not per-account allowances.** One
   tenant's viral post can consume the app-wide hourly budget and halt comment sync
   for every other tenant. That makes fair-share allocation a multi-tenancy
   availability concern (§7), not just a scheduling nicety.

**Onboarding consequence:** platform N+1 is not purely "implement the provider
interface and add a registry row" (§3.1, §8.1). Some platforms carry a quota or
business-process gate that must clear before the platform can be enabled at all. The
capability registry needs a quota/feasibility dimension alongside its capability
flags.

### D. Comment size — limits and typical length

Two different numbers doing two different jobs. The **maximum** feeds validation and
the capability registry (§3.1); the **typical** feeds storage sizing. Conflating them
would either reject valid comments or over-provision disk by an order of magnitude.

**Maximum length per platform** — all [measured] from platform docs or Blotato's own
documentation:

| Platform | Comment max | Note |
|---|---|---|
| YouTube | **10,000** | most generous |
| Facebook | **8,000** | via current-api.md |
| Instagram | **2,200** | via current-api.md |
| LinkedIn | **1,250** | verified March 2026; some sources say up to 1,750 |
| X / Twitter | **280** free tier, 25,000 Premium | replies are posts, so post limits apply |
| Threads | ~500 | [speculation] inferred from Threads' post limit |
| **TikTok** | **150** | **~50x tighter than YouTube — see the consequence below** |

**Typical length** [measured], and the headline is that real comments are very short:

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| YouTube corpus | mean **~112 chars** in the most discursive category; variance is enormous (σ ≈ 1,605 in Comedy) |
| Instagram study | **75% under 50 chars** for music/show/sport; politics reaches P75 ≈ 83 |
| X | median conversational message **~38 chars** |

**Planning figure: ~80 characters mean**, roughly 100 bytes once emoji are counted at
4 bytes each. The distribution is heavily right-skewed like everything else here, so
the mean sits well above the median.

#### Consequence 1 — TikTok breaks the reply-generation budget

The Content Generator assumes ~65 output tokens per reply, which is **~260 characters**.
That fits Instagram, Facebook, LinkedIn, and YouTube comfortably. It is **~1.7x over
TikTok's 150-character cap**, and sits right on the boundary of X's free-tier 280.

So reply length is not a single global budget — it is per-platform, and TikTok needs
roughly half the output allowance (~37 tokens). Two things follow: `maxCommentLength`
in the capability registry must be enforced *before* the model call, as a prompt
constraint, not only after as validation — otherwise every TikTok reply is generated,
paid for, and then rejected. And the cost model's per-reply token figure is a blended
average across platforms rather than a constant.

#### Consequence 2 — storage per comment row is ~800B, not ~500B

Text is the small part. The row is dominated by identifiers and, above all, by indexes:

| Component | Bytes | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Identifier and scalar fields | ~276 | six cuid FKs at ~26B each, two platform ids, timestamps, status, counters |
| Postgres tuple header + null bitmap + item pointer | ~30 | |
| `text` | ~105 | ~80 chars, emoji-weighted |
| **Row subtotal** | **~410** | |
| **Six index entries** | **~305** | PK, one unique, four secondary indexes |
| **Total** | **~715B, call it ~750B** | |

**Indexes are ~43% of the `Comment` table's footprint**, which is the non-obvious result
and the one worth acting on. A *full* index over this table runs ~64 GB at the 2-year
figures, which is why the idempotency index is **partial** — only client-supplied
outbound replies carry a key, so it covers a few million rows rather than ~9.6×10^8, and
inbound ingestion never touches it. Every insert maintains one b-tree per matching index,
so write amplification, not row width, is what index count actually costs.

### 2. Retrieval — a policy output, not a volume derivation

**This is the block that answers "how many platform calls do we make," and it is the
one number in this plan that is chosen rather than derived.** It follows post count
and the polling schedule; it does not follow comment volume. The ceiling comes first
below, because it is the denominator every polling option is judged against — and it
is not the number the platform advertises.

#### The ceiling: why the nominal number is not the usable one

The headline figure is 200 calls/hour/user. That is not what the system actually gets
to spend, and the gap between nominal and effective is large enough to change which
polling design is acceptable. Four haircuts apply, in order:

| Step | 2-year value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal: 200 calls/hr × connected accounts | ~5,556/sec | [measured] Meta Platform Rate Limit formula |
| × daily-active fraction (~30%) | ~1,667/sec | [speculation on the fraction, [measured] on the mechanism] the formula counts **daily active** users, not connected accounts — see below |
| × safety margin (80%) | ~1,333/sec | [policy] never plan to 100% of a rate limit |
| × share available to comments (~50%) | **~667/sec** | [speculation] the pool is shared with post publishing and account sync — **comments are a new consumer of an already-partly-spent budget** |

**Effective ceiling: ~667/sec at the 2-year target, versus a nominal ~5,556/sec** — a
factor of ~8 between what the docs advertise and what the system can spend.

#### The polling designs, measured against that ceiling

Presenting the rejected option alongside the adopted one is deliberate: the gap
between them is the single most load-bearing design decision in this plan, and against
the ceiling above the rejected option turns out to be disallowed rather than merely
expensive.

| Polling design | Now | 2-year | Utilization of the ~667/sec effective ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat 1000s poll, 45-day window (~3,888 polls/post) | ~1,500/sec | **~15,000/sec** | ❌ **~22x over — rejected, disallowed not merely costly** |
| Backoff + target-yield, 45-day window (~110 polls/post) | ~43/sec | **~430/sec** | ✅ **~64% — adopted, but thinner than it looks** |
| Backoff, 14-day poll window (~47 polls/post) | ~18/sec | ~185/sec | 🎯 **~28% — the real target** |

Sizing against the flat-poll row is what inflates every downstream figure in §1.4. The
two mechanisms that replace it are below; §1.4 applies them and does not restate them.

##### Mechanism 1 — adaptive interval: target-yield while active, geometric backoff when quiet

**The insight both regimes share: a poll costs the same whether it returns 50 comments
or zero.** One API call is one unit of quota regardless of payload, and a page returns
up to 100 comments. So the metric to maximize is *comments returned per call*, and a
flat clock interval optimizes nothing — it spends the same quota on a two-hour-old
post mid-burst as on a five-week-old post that will never receive another comment.

**While a post is active**, set the interval from observed comment velocity rather
than a fixed tick:

```
interval = target_yield / observed_velocity,   clamped to [5 min, 12 h]
```

with `target_yield` around 10-20 comments. A post receiving 20 comments/hour gets
polled roughly every 30-60 minutes; one receiving 2/hour gets polled every 5-10 hours.
Both return a useful batch. Velocity is a decaying average over recent polls, so it
adapts without needing a model of the post's age.

**Once polls come back empty**, switch to geometric backoff: double the interval each
time, capped at 12 hours. On any poll that returns new comments, do **not** snap back
to the floor — recompute from the observed velocity instead. Snapping to the floor
makes a post that receives one straggler comment per day oscillate between the 5-minute
floor and the cap forever, which is the failure mode that would quietly eat the budget.

**Jitter is not optional here, for a reason specific to this product.** A scheduling
tool publishes many posts at the same scheduled minute, so a deterministic doubling
schedule would keep all of them phase-aligned for their entire lifetime, turning steady
load into synchronized spikes every 5 minutes, then 10, then 20. Add ±20% randomization
to every computed interval so the fleet decorrelates.

**Where the ~110 polls/post figure comes from:**

| Phase | Window | Interval behavior | Polls | Share of the post's comments |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Active | 0-48h | target-yield, 5 min floor | ~15 | ~90% |
| Decay ramp | 48h-~69h | 5m, 10m, 20m, 40m, 80m, 160m, 320m, 640m | ~8 | the remainder |
| Dormant | ~day 3 to day 45 | 12h cap | ~84 | ≈ 0 |
| **Total** | | | **~107** | |

Against ~3,888 polls under a flat 1000s interval, that is a **~35x reduction**, and it
is where the ~430/sec in the table above comes from: `3.9 posts/sec × ~110 polls`.

##### Mechanism 2 — decouple the polling window from the retention window

**Read the last two rows of that ledger together: ~84 of ~107 polls, roughly 76% of
all remaining traffic, happen in the dormant phase and return nothing.** Backoff
already reduced each one's frequency to the 12-hour cap; what it cannot do is stop
paying for a post that will never receive another comment, because a capped interval
still costs 2 polls/day for 42 more days.

The plan conflates two windows that have no reason to be equal:

| Window | What it governs | Current | Proposed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Retention** | how long comments stay queryable in our database | 45 days | 45 days, unchanged |
| **Polling** | how long we keep spending platform quota on a post | 45 days | **~14 days** |

Retention is a storage and compliance decision (§6.2). Polling is a quota decision.
Tying them together was an accident of both defaulting to Blotato's documented 45-day
figure.

Cutting the polling window to 14 days leaves the active and ramp phases untouched and
shortens only the dormant tail:

```
~15 active + ~8 ramp + ~24 dormant (12 days x 2/day) = ~47 polls/post
3.9 posts/sec x ~47 = ~185 retrieval calls/sec
```

**The tradeoff, stated plainly:** comments arriving on a post after day 14 are not
captured. Given that ~90% of a post's comments arrive in the first 48 hours, this
trades a sliver of long-tail completeness for ~57% of all retrieval quota. It also
sits comfortably inside Blotato's existing product stance, which already tells
customers it does not backfill comments (current-api.md).

**Two safety valves make the short window defensible rather than merely cheap**, since
the real risk is a post resurfacing late through algorithmic recommendation:

- **Re-awakening.** Because the post row and its comments persist to 45 days
  regardless, a dormant post can be returned to the active schedule at any time —
  triggered by a webhook arriving for it, by the customer opening it in the UI, or by
  an explicit re-subscribe call. Nothing is lost permanently, only paused.
- **Per-user override.** The polling window is a policy value, so a customer who
  genuinely needs 45-day coverage can have it, paid for out of their own budget share
  rather than the shared default.

**Why the haircuts change the verdict and not just the arithmetic.** Against the
nominal ceiling the adopted design sits at a comfortable ~8% and the 14-day poll
window reads as an optional optimization. Against the effective ceiling it is **~64%**,
which leaves little room for the burst factor, for an uneven account distribution, or
for publishing traffic growing its own share. Shortening the poll window moves it to
~28% and is therefore **load-bearing, not optional** — the single highest-value change
remaining in this section.

#### Why "daily active" is the load-bearing word

Meta's wording is **"the number of individual daily active users of an app"**
[measured]. Not accounts that authorized the app, not accounts holding a valid token.
The vendor summaries put it bluntly: 100 engaged users buys 20,000 calls an hour,
100,000 dormant signups buys nothing.

**The structural problem this creates for a scheduling product specifically:**

| | Scales with | For this product |
|---|---|---|
| Quota pool | **daily active users** | users who opened the app or transacted today |
| Comment-polling workload | **tracked posts under a 45-day window** | posts scheduled at any point in the last 45 days, whether or not the user came back |

These two decouple, and they decouple *badly* here. A user batch-schedules a month of
content on Monday and does not return for three weeks. Their posts are polled for
comments the entire time, contributing full workload, while contributing to the quota
pool on only the days they were active. The workload tracks something closer to
monthly actives; the pool tracks daily actives. The gap between them is roughly the
product's DAU/MAU ratio, typically **~20-30% for a B2B tool** [speculation], which is
where the 30% haircut comes from.

**The uncomfortable corollary:** the better this product is at its core promise —
schedule once, walk away — the lower DAU goes, the smaller the quota pool gets, and
the workload does not change. *Succeeding at the value proposition actively shrinks
the API budget.* That is worth stating plainly because it is counterintuitive and
because no amount of engineering fixes it.

**One documented mitigation, which may substantially soften this** [measured]: Meta
states that "in cases where there are strong fluctuations in daily usage, such as an
app with low activity on weekdays, the number of users for your app is calculated
based on weekly and monthly active users." A scheduling tool has exactly that
fluctuation profile, so Meta may well compute the pool on a WAU or MAU basis, which
would push the haircut from ~30% toward ~100% and remove most of the problem.

**This is therefore a 3x uncertainty band on the plan's most load-bearing number**,
and it is not resolvable from documentation:

| Basis Meta actually applies | Effective ceiling | Adopted-design utilization |
|---|---|---|
| Daily active (pessimistic) | ~667/sec | **~64%** |
| Monthly active (optimistic) | ~2,200/sec | **~20%** |

**Resolve it by measurement, not estimation.** Meta returns an `X-App-Usage` header
carrying call count as a *percentage of the app's current limit*, and
`X-Business-Use-Case-Usage` for the BUC equivalent. Those let the real pool size be
back-calculated in production without knowing which basis Meta chose. Until then the
design should be safe under the pessimistic branch, which is the argument for treating
the 14-day poll window as load-bearing.

One question the docs do not settle: whether an API call the system makes *on behalf
of* a user marks that user active for the formula. If it does, polling is partly
self-sustaining and the haircut mostly vanishes. If it does not, dormant accounts are
pure workload with no quota contribution. Assume the latter until the headers say
otherwise.

**Dormancy cuts the same way under BUC, by a different mechanism.** Instagram's BUC is
`4800 × impressions in the last 24 hours` per app-user pair; Facebook Pages' is
`4800 × engaged users`. Both are near zero for a dormant or low-reach account. So a
quiet account is penalized twice over — it adds nothing to the shared Platform pool
*and* its own BUC allowance collapses — while its 45-day-old posts still queue for
polling.

**That yields a clean design rule** (§3.3, §1.4): account dormancy should be a direct
input to polling priority. A post belonging to an account with no recent impressions
has both the lowest expected comment yield and the least available quota, so those two
signals point the same direction. Deprioritize it aggressively rather than treating
every watched post as equally due.

#### Three further properties of this ceiling

- **It is a shared app-wide pool, not a per-account allowance.** One tenant's viral
  post can consume the app's hourly budget and halt comment sync for every other
  tenant. That makes fair-share allocation a multi-tenancy availability concern (§7),
  not only a scheduling one.
- **Comments compete with the core publishing product for the same quota.** This is
  the haircut most easily missed: Blotato already spends this budget on posting and
  account sync. A comment system does not arrive at an empty pool. The ~50% share
  above is a placeholder for a number only production telemetry can supply.
- **Utilization is invariant to platform mix.** If Meta is only 40% of the comment
  fleet, both Meta demand and the Meta pool shrink by the same 40%, so the percentage
  is unchanged. The mix does not rescue a design that is over budget. Note that
  **today the mix is 100% Meta** — Instagram and Facebook are the only platforms
  supporting comments at all (current-api.md), so the figures above are the current
  reality, not a worst case.
- **Business Use Case limits override the Platform limit where both apply**, which
  they do for Instagram and Facebook Page content. BUC is per app-user pair rather
  than pooled, and impression-driven — covered above under dormancy. The practical
  consequence for this section: the ceiling is not one number but two mechanisms with
  different shapes, so per-account budget must be **observed at runtime** via the
  `X-App-Usage` and `X-Business-Use-Case-Usage` headers rather than configured as a
  constant (§3.3). BUC is additionally capped by CPU and total processing time, also
  reported in that header.


**Consequence for §1.4's framing.** Once retrieval is bounded by a per-account budget
(§3.3) rather than chasing a throughput target, "how many instances hit 15,000/sec"
stops being a question that arises. The capacity question becomes "is the aggregate
platform budget enough to keep staleness acceptable," which is answerable from the
ceiling derivation above: at ~64% of the effective ceiling, yes but without much
margin — which is what makes the 14-day poll window load-bearing rather than optional.

### 3. Infrastructure — follows from blocks 1 and 2

| Metric | Now | 2-year | Derivation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concurrent outbound connections | ~13 | **~130** | 430/sec × ~300ms avg call latency |
| LLM calls/sec | ~4.8 | **~49** | ~1 reply per call — per-account batching does not fill a batch at these volumes, see §1.4 |
| LLM concurrent in-flight | ~11 | **~113** | calls/sec × ~2.3s per unbatched call |
| LLM tokens/month, uncached | ~8×10^9 | **~8×10^10** | replies/month × ~615 tokens (400 preamble + 150 in + 65 out, unamortized) |
| LLM tokens/month, prompt-cached | ~3×10^9 | **~3×10^10** | preamble is byte-identical per user; ~255 effective tokens/reply |
| LLM cost/month at $0.50-2/M | ~$1.6k-6.5k | **~$16k-65k** | cached figure; ~$39k-156k without caching. ≈ $0.16-0.65 per account/month |
| Query rate, all pollers | ~15/sec | ~150/sec | claim + assembly queries across the three loops |

**Every instance count in this plan is now a redundancy decision, not a capacity
one.** No service exceeds one instance's throughput at the 2-year figures: ~130
concurrent connections and ~65 in-flight LLM calls both sit well inside a single
process. The 3-instance baseline per service (§1.4) is for surviving a failure with
headroom, and nothing more.

### 4. Cross-checks against external ceilings

The figures above are only meaningful if the platforms permit them. Read and write
checks pass at the 2-year target and both failed under the previous inputs; YouTube
does not pass at any inputs:

| Check | 2-year demand | Platform limit | Utilization |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instagram comment **writes** | ~1.8/hour/account | 60 writes/hour/user | **~3%** |
| Meta **reads**, effective pool after haircuts | ~430/sec | ~667/sec effective (~5,556 nominal) | **~64%** |
| Meta **reads**, with 14-day poll window | ~185/sec | ~667/sec effective | **~28%** |
| YouTube reads | ~2.3M calls/day | 10,000 units/day per project | ❌ **~230x over — see §C** |

The Instagram write check is the independent confirmation that the old 75% reply rate
was never deliverable: it would have sat at ~52 writes/hour/account against a hard cap
of 60, at 87% utilization assuming perfectly even distribution across accounts, which
never holds. YouTube remains infeasible on default quota at any poll interval and is
gated on a quota-increase audit, not on engineering (§C).

### 5. Levers, if the ceiling binds

Adopted-design utilization sits at ~64% of the effective Meta ceiling, which is
workable but not roomy. Four levers exist, and **the first two are easily conflated** —
one buys quota, the other buys only latency.

**1. Webhooks — the only lever that actually buys quota.** Meta supports comment
webhooks on Instagram and Page feed webhooks on Facebook. Webhook deliveries are
pushed to us and **do not consume the rate limit**, so subscribed posts collapse from
a poll schedule to occasional reconciliation-only polling. Nothing else on this list
changes the numerator that way. §3.2 currently descopes webhooks to a stub route with
`CommentSyncState.nextPollAfter` as the hook; the ceiling analysis above promotes them
from a nice-to-have to **the primary scaling lever**, and that reprioritization is the
main thing this section exists to record. The take-home still ships the stub — this is
a statement about which direction production goes, not a change of scope.

**2. Batching — buys latency and connections, not quota.** Meta's Graph API batch
endpoint accepts up to 50 sub-requests, but
**each sub-request is counted separately against the rate limit** [measured]. A batch
of 10 calls costs 10 calls. `GET /photos?ids=4,5,6` costs three. So batching reduces
round trips and concurrent connections — real wins against the ~130-connection figure
in block 3 — while leaving the quota position in block 4 completely unchanged. It is
not a remedy for being over the ceiling.

Which platforms allow it at all, since it varies and only Facebook offers true
multi-post batching:

| Platform | Multiple posts in 1 request? | Best bulk mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Facebook** | ✅ Yes, via Graph API batch requests | Batch multiple Graph calls |
| **Instagram** | ❌ Usually one media/post at a time | Parallel requests |
| **TikTok** | ❌ Normal comments endpoint = one video | Parallel requests |
| **YouTube** | ❌ `videoId` is single-video | Parallel requests (channel-wide comments listing was deprecated to curb spam scraping) |
| **LinkedIn** | ❌ Generally post-scoped | Parallel requests |
| **X** | ⚠️ Depends on endpoint/access | Search/query-based approaches |

Design against the worst case — parallel per-post requests — with per-platform
batching as an optimization where available.

**3. Shorten the polling window.** Decoupling the poll window from the 45-day
retention window moves utilization from ~64% to ~28% (block 2). Free, already
derived, and the highest-value change not yet applied.

**4. Request a higher ceiling.** The sanctioned path: a limit increase via the app
dashboard or a Meta partner manager, and the equivalent quota audit for YouTube. Slow
and non-technical, but it is the only remedy for YouTube, which no engineering lever
reaches.

**Explicitly not a lever: registering additional App IDs.** Rate limits are per app,
so a second App ID is a second pool — but splitting users across two apps gains
nothing, since pool size scales linearly with users. The version that would raise
capacity, the same users authorizing both apps, is circumvention that Meta prohibits,
and it would require duplicate App Review and Business Verification plus a second
OAuth consent per user. Recorded here so it is not rediscovered as an idea later.
