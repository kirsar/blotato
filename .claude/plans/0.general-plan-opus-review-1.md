## The headline problem

The plan is 833 lines of settled reasoning with zero code written, and the four
questions that actually gate writing code are the only ones still open.
`0.general-plan.md` sets a scope guardrail saying a reviewer should read this in well
under an hour, then spends the next 700 lines violating it. Capacity planning,
sharding strategy, and Citus are fully resolved. Whether the job runner is real code
is not. That priority is inverted for a take-home.

More seriously, the entire infrastructure sizing rests on one input that the
document's own data contradicts.

| Input | Plan's value | Plan's own evidence, same file |
|---|---|---|
| Comments per post | 500, applied to every post | P50 sum across platforms is ~57; per-platform P50 is 5 to 30 |
| Reply rate | 75% of all inbound comments | no product or platform basis given |
| Poll interval | flat 1000s for 45 days | ~3,880 of ~3,888 polls per post return nothing |

The comments-per-post distribution table sits twenty lines above the capacity table
that ignores it. Five hundred is roughly a P95 to P99 figure, and it is applied as
the mean. Everything downstream inherits a 25x inflation.

| Derived figure | Plan's number | Revised, derived in R1-R3 below |
|---|---|---|
| Comments/sec ingested | 1,930 | ~98 |
| Retrieval calls/sec | 15,000 | ~430 |
| Storage at 45 days | 6.6 TB | ~250 GB |
| Worker dynos | 16 | ~9-12, all redundancy-driven |

The arithmetic is all correct. The growth compounding, the seconds-per-month
conversions, the concurrency products, and the storage rollups all check out. The
inputs are the problem, not the math.

Three consequences worth naming directly:

- **The 15,000 due-checks/sec figure is the least defensible number and drives the
  largest infrastructure count.** It means polling every tracked post every sixteen
  minutes for forty-five days, for posts whose comment activity is concentrated in
  the first forty-eight hours. Adaptive interval and backoff-on-empty are listed
  under "how to scale past the ceiling." They belong in the baseline design, not as
  a future lever.
- **No cross-check against platform rate limits.** Section 3.3 acknowledges
  per-account limits exist, then never tests the sizing against them. At a few
  hundred calls per hour per account, which is the right order for the Meta Graph
  API, one hundred thousand accounts caps aggregate throughput below the 15,000/sec
  the plan sizes for. The external limit is the real constraint, and it is lower
  than the computed need. That inverts the design: the poller should spend a
  per-account budget by priority, not chase a throughput target.
- **No LLM cost estimate anywhere.** For a system whose central bet is generated
  replies, cost per reply is the number that decides whether the product exists. At
  the plan's own token figures the input volume alone runs into the millions of
  tokens per minute, which no standard provider tier serves, and the monthly spend
  lands in a range that would dominate revenue at 10^5 users. Section 4.5 correctly
  calls this the tightest bottleneck, then answers it with three instances. Instance
  count is irrelevant against a provider quota.

### Secondary numeric notes

- The Content Generator sizing uses ~20 comments per batch for token budgeting and
  ~15 replies per batch for latency. Pick one figure.
- ~50 output tokens/sec is slow for 2026-era models; 100-200 is more typical, which
  roughly halves the ~20s per batch call and the ~2,000 concurrent-call figure that
  follows from it.
- ~1,000 sustainable concurrent connections per instance is asserted with no basis,
  and it sets the five-instance floor. On a shared-CPU dyno with 512MB to 1GB of
  RAM, holding a thousand in-flight HTTPS responses each up to 500 comments of JSON
  is not obviously achievable.
- The API service load figure is stated in posts/sec, which is the creation rate.
  The plan separately argues reads dominate. There is no read-rate estimate anywhere,
  so the service whose load is claimed to be read-dominated is sized on writes.
- The read-heavy versus write-heavy reconciliation paragraph in the Fermi section
  reads as an argument with a previous reviewer rather than a conclusion. Symptom of
  the document preserving the history of its own revisions.

## Recommended replacements for the load-bearing inputs

This part answers the headline problem rather than restating it. Four inputs are
replaced and the whole sizing chain is re-derived from them. High-level only; none of
this prescribes implementation.

### R1. Comments per post: three numbers doing three different jobs

The plan collapses one number into three roles. Separate them.

First, a mean column, which the distribution table lacks. Means are recoverable from
the published averages the plan already cites, and fittable from the measured
percentiles for the rest.

| Platform | P50 | **Mean** | P90 | P99 | Mean basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Instagram | ~10 | **20** | ~80 | ~600 | published average, Socialinsider 2025 |
| TikTok | ~30 | **50** | ~250 | ~2,000 | published average |
| Facebook | ~5 | **22** | ~40 | ~400 | published average |
| LinkedIn | 6 | **~28** | 75 | 354 | lognormal fit to measured P50/P99 |
| X / Twitter | ~0-1 | **1** | ~5 | ~50 | published average |
| Threads | ~1 | **~6** | ~12 | ~100 | fitted, Instagram-adjacent |
| YouTube | ~5 | **~35** | ~60 | ~700 | fitted to P50/P90/P99 |
| Equal-weight blend | | **~23** | | | |

Methodological correction the plan needs regardless: its existing table has a **Sum**
row across percentile columns. Percentiles do not sum. The P90 of a mixed population
is not the sum of per-platform P90s, and the ~4,204 figure in the P99 row is not a
meaningful quantity. The mean column is the only column on which arithmetic is valid,
which is a second reason to add it.

**Selection effect that pushes the planning mean up.** Section 0 chooses tier 2,
opt-in per-post watching. Watched posts are not a random sample of all posts; a user
opts in a post they expect engagement on. Apply roughly 1.5x to the blend.

**Recommendation, three numbers:**

**Revised upward on new evidence.** A second measured population exists and it is much
higher than the platform-wide blend: Respondology's 2025 report covers 118.4M comments
across 734,500 posts from 450+ brands, which works out to **~161 comments/post for
brand accounts**. Platform-wide is ~23; brand accounts are ~7x that. The mean therefore
depends almost entirely on customer mix. Blotato's self-serve positioning sits nearer
the platform figure, pushed up by opt-in selection and by paying accounts engaging
above the median.

| Number | Value | Job it does |
|---|---|---|
| Planning mean | **~50 comments/post** | steady-state sizing: instance counts, storage, write rate, LLM cost |
| Enterprise-tenant mean | **~161** | the segment ceiling, what a large-brand tenant looks like |
| Burst factor | **3-5x mean** | provisioning headroom for diurnal peaks and the occasional viral post |
| Per-post cap | **500, kept as-is** | pagination bound, single-fetch memory bound, stop-syncing policy |

So 500 is not wrong, it is mislabeled. It is a per-post ceiling, and the error is
using a per-post ceiling as a fleet-wide multiplier. Keep it, relabel it, and stop
multiplying by it. Note also that 500 as a *cap* is a real product decision with a
visible consequence: a post exceeding it has comments the customer cannot see. That
deserves one sentence stating it is accepted.

### R2. Reply rate: an eligible fraction, not a policy target

Adopt the range from the speculation, which is more defensible than 50%/75% because
it is grounded in comment-content distribution rather than ambition.

| Account type | Substantive-reply share |
|---|---|
| Discussion-driving, high-engagement | 35-40% |
| Typical, mixed content | **20-30%, plan at 25%** |
| Broadcast, meme, announcement | 10-15% |

Two reframings matter more than the number itself.

- **Eligible fraction is not reply rate.** Actual replies sent equal the minimum of
  what is eligible and what the per-account budget and cost ceiling permit. The plan
  treats 75% as something the system delivers; it is at most something the system is
  allowed to attempt. State it as a filter output, and let R4's budget be the binding
  term.
- **The platform settles this independently of policy.** Instagram caps
  `/media/comments` at **60 writes per hour per user**. The plan's 1,448 replies/sec
  across ~10^5 accounts is ~52 writes/hour/account, or 87% of that hard cap with zero
  headroom and assuming perfectly even distribution across accounts, which never
  holds. The revised ~49/sec lands at ~1.8/hour, under 3%. 75% was not a policy the
  system could have executed.
- **One measured anchor exists and it bounds the other side.** Respondology finds
  ~20% of comments across 118.4M samples are toxic, spam, or off-brand. Those want
  moderation, not a reply, so that is a floor on the not-eligible share. The
  reply-eligible figure itself remains unsourced and should stay marked as
  speculation.
- **The filter is baseline, not a scaling lever.** Section 4.5 lists "filter
  low-signal comments" as the first thing to do when the provider ceiling is hit.
  It should run from day one, because it removes 70-80% of volume before the
  expensive call, and because it does not need a frontier model to do it. Length
  thresholds, emoji-only detection, absence of a question mark or entity mention, and
  a small classifier get most of the way. This is a 4x cost reduction available at
  near-zero cost, which makes it the highest-leverage single decision in the pipeline.

### R3. Backoff: a derived figure to replace 15,000/sec

The 15,000/sec figure comes from flat 1000s polling across a 45-day window, giving
~3,888 polls per post of which nearly all return nothing. Replace the flat interval
with two mechanisms.

**Mechanism one, target-yield polling while active.** Set the interval so a poll
expects to return a useful batch rather than a fixed clock tick. Interval equals
target yield divided by observed comment velocity, clamped to a floor and a ceiling.
At a planning mean of 25 comments concentrated in the first 48 hours, a target yield
of ~10 comments per poll means only a handful of productive polls per post, with the
floor binding only during an early burst.

**Mechanism two, geometric backoff on consecutive empty polls.** Double the interval
after each empty poll, cap it, and reset on any poll that returns new comments.

Poll count over a post's life, doubling from 5 minutes to a 12-hour cap:

| Phase | Interval behavior | Polls |
|---|---|---|
| Active, first ~48h | target-yield, 5 min floor | ~15 |
| Decay ramp | 5m, 10m, 20m, 40m, 80m, 160m, 320m, 640m | ~8, spanning ~21h |
| Dormant | 12h cap, remaining ~42 days | ~84 |
| **Total** | | **~110** |

Against 3,888 flat, that is a **~35x reduction**. Steady-state retrieval rate is the
post arrival rate times polls per post:

```
3.9 posts/sec x ~110 polls/post = ~430 retrieval calls/sec
```

**~430/sec replaces ~15,000/sec.**

**The biggest remaining lever is free: decouple the polling window from the retention
window.** The plan conflates them at 45 days. Retention is how long comments are
*stored*; it has no bearing on how long a post is *watched*. A post with no new
comments in a week will almost certainly not get more. Stop polling at ~14 days while
still storing for 45:

```
~15 active + ~8 ramp + ~24 dormant = ~47 polls/post
3.9 x 47 = ~185 retrieval calls/sec
```

The dormant phase is 84 of 110 polls, so this single change removes most of what
remains. Recommend ~430/sec as the conservative sizing figure and ~185/sec as the
target once the poll window is separated from retention.

**Downstream collapse.** Concurrency is rate times call duration:

```
430/sec x ~300ms = ~130 concurrent outbound connections
```

Against ~4,500. That fits inside a single instance, so Scheduler-Retriever drops from
seven instances to a redundancy baseline of three. The largest infrastructure number
in the plan was entirely an artifact of the flat poll interval.

**The validation that matters most.** Meta's Graph API Platform Rate Limit is
`200 x number of daily active users` over a rolling 60-minute window, assessed at the
**app level as a shared pool**, not as a per-account allowance. Verified against
[Meta's rate-limiting documentation](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/graph-api/overview/rate-limiting/).
Because both demand and budget scale with the same account count, the comparison is
still cleanest expressed per account, and the platform mix cancels out:

| Polling design | Calls/hour/account | Against the 200/hour/account pooled equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Flat 1000s | ~540 | **infeasible, ~2.7x over** |
| Backoff, 45-day window | ~15 | ~8% utilization |
| Backoff, 14-day window | ~7 | ~4% utilization |

**These are against the nominal 200/hour and therefore optimistic.** The usable
ceiling is lower once three haircuts apply: Meta counts *daily active* users rather
than total connected accounts, a safety margin is standard, and the pool is shared
with post publishing and account sync, so comments arrive at an already-partly-spent
budget. Working those through gives roughly a third of the nominal figure, which moves
flat polling from ~2.7x over to **~22x over**, and moves the adopted design from a
comfortable ~8% to **~64%**. The derivation is in `0.general-plan.md`, Capacity
planning, "Deriving the Meta ceiling." The consequence is that the 14-day poll window
is load-bearing rather than an optional optimization.

This is the real argument for backoff, stronger than the cost argument. Flat polling
was not merely expensive, it was not permitted by the platforms. Backoff is what makes
the design feasible at all, and that is the sentence the plan should carry.

Two caveats on that 200 figure, both of which the plan should record rather than
inherit silently:

- **Business Use Case limits override Platform limits where both apply**, and for
  Instagram and Facebook Page content they generally do. Those are formula-driven and
  dynamic rather than a flat constant. Treat 200 as an order-of-magnitude anchor good
  enough to kill flat polling, not as the governing number for this workload.
- **The pool is app-wide, which makes fair-share allocation load-bearing rather than
  a nicety.** See R4.

**YouTube is a separate and far harder constraint, and the plan does not mention it.**
The Data API v3 default quota is 10,000 units per day **per project**, with
`commentThreads.list` costing 1 unit per page. That is a fixed ceiling for the entire
application across all customers, and it does not scale with user count the way Meta's
does.

| Design | YouTube calls/day needed | Against 10,000 units/day |
|---|---|---|
| Flat 1000s polling | ~5.3M | ~530x over |
| Backoff, 14-day window | ~2.3M | ~230x over |
| One single poll per post, ever | ~48,000 | **still ~5x over** |

The last row is the important one. **No polling interval makes YouTube feasible on
default quota**, because the post arrival rate alone exceeds the daily budget. The
only remedies are a quota-increase audit from Google or a push-based ingestion path.
This has a real design consequence the plan misses entirely: platform onboarding is
not purely "implement the provider interface and add a registry row." Some platforms
carry a business-process gate that must clear before the platform can be enabled at
all. That belongs in the capability registry as a quota or feasibility dimension, and
in section 8's onboarding story as a step that is not code.

### R4. Designing for a per-account budget

The reframe: **polling is not a schedule, it is a budget allocation problem.** The
scheduler does not have a throughput target to hit. It has a fixed external call
budget per account per window, and its job is to spend that budget on the posts most
likely to yield new comments. Once framed this way, the 15,000/sec worst case stops
being a number to provision against and becomes a question that no longer arises.

Design elements, high level:

1. **Two budget scopes, not one, because the real limits are pooled.** Meta's quota is
   app-wide over a rolling hour and YouTube's is project-wide per day, so the primary
   accounting unit is a shared token bucket per platform, with a per-account
   allocation carved out of it. A purely per-account counter cannot enforce a pooled
   ceiling. Configured values are initial estimates only.
2. **Reads and writes share one budget.** On most platforms, retrieval calls and
   reply-posting calls draw on the same per-account quota. The plan sizes
   Scheduler-Retriever and Reply Publisher independently, as if their limits were
   separate. They are not. Reserve an explicit slice for writes.
3. **Writes preempt reads.** A delayed reply is user-visible; a delayed poll is not.
   When budget is scarce, queued replies go first.
4. **Priority score instead of a due time.** Rank a post by expected new comments,
   roughly observed velocity times time since last poll, decayed by post age. Spend
   the budget top-down. This subsumes the backoff schedule in R3 rather than sitting
   beside it: backoff is what the score produces for a quiet post.
5. **A fair-share floor, at two levels.** Within an account, reserve part of the
   allocation for round-robin coverage so one high-velocity post cannot starve the
   others. Across accounts, cap each tenant's draw on the shared pool. The second is
   the more important of the two and is a direct consequence of the pooled quota
   model: without it, one large customer's viral post consumes the app-wide hourly
   budget and every other tenant's comment sync stops. That is a cross-tenant
   availability failure, which makes it a multi-tenancy concern belonging in section
   7 as much as a scheduling one.
6. **Budget exhaustion is a normal state, not an error.** When an account's budget is
   spent, its posts wait. This is the explicit place where staleness is accepted, and
   it is consistent with section 0 choosing a non-real-time tier. It should be
   surfaced to the customer as a freshness signal rather than hidden.
7. **Observe the budget, do not just configure it.** Track 429s and remaining-quota
   headers where platforms expose them, and correct the estimate at runtime. Meta's
   limits in particular scale with audience size, so a static per-account constant
   will be wrong in both directions.

**Architectural consequence worth flagging now.** A budget-aware scheduler cannot use
the plan's claim query, which selects the globally-oldest due posts under a single
limit. That would let one worker claim a batch dominated by posts belonging to
budget-exhausted accounts. Claiming has to become account-aware, either by selecting
accounts with budget first and posts within them second, or by partitioning workers
across accounts so each owns a disjoint set of budgets. Both have real consequences
for the database-as-queue pattern, and this is where that pattern first gets genuinely
hard. It is the design question most worth resolving before code.

Two structural conclusions follow, and both simplify the plan substantially.

- **Every instance count becomes a redundancy decision, not a capacity decision.** No
  service needs more than one instance for throughput. That is a cleaner and more
  honest story than the current per-service capacity derivations, and it removes the
  need for most of section 1.4's length.
- **Section 5.6 can be deleted.** At ~146 write rows/sec and ~250 GB, a single
  Postgres instance is not close to any wall. Sharding, Citus, and a Kafka ingestion
  buffer are answers to a problem the corrected inputs do not produce. Keep one
  sentence naming partitioning as the retention-cleanup mechanism, because
  partition-drop beats row-by-row delete regardless of scale, and drop the rest.

### LLM cost, which the plan never computed

Now computable, and it is the number that decides whether the product is viable.
Blended ~240 tokens per reply, from the plan's own figures of ~150 input tokens per
comment, ~65 output, and ~400 fixed overhead amortized across a batch.

| Scenario | Replies/month | Tokens/month | Cost at $0.50-2/M | Per account/month |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plan's inputs | 3.75×10^9 | ~9×10^11 | **$450k-1.8M** | **$4.50-18** |
| Revised inputs | 1.27×10^8 | ~3×10^10 | **$15k-61k** | **$0.15-0.61** |

The plan's own numbers describe a product whose inference cost alone exceeds
plausible per-seat revenue. The revised numbers describe a line item. This single
table is probably the most valuable thing the capacity section could contain, and its
absence is the largest analytical gap in the current document.

One product-level caution to state explicitly: auto-replying to 75% of inbound
comments is not only expensive, it is behavior platforms penalize as inauthentic
engagement. The 25% figure is defensible on cost, on platform-limit, and on
account-safety grounds simultaneously.

## Concrete design and schema problems

These are real bugs, not sizing quibbles.

- **The unique constraints break multi-tenancy.** In `5.storage.md`, `SocialAccount`
  is unique on platform plus platform account id, so an agency and its client cannot
  both connect the same Instagram account. `Post` is unique on platform plus platform
  post id, so two workspaces cannot track the same public post. Same for `Comment`.
  This directly contradicts section 7.2. All three need the workspace in the key.
- **Row-level security is claimed but not implementable as designed.** `Comment` has
  no workspace column. Every policy check would join through `SocialAccount` on the
  hottest table in the system. If RLS is the defense-in-depth story, denormalize the
  workspace id onto `Comment`.
- **Incremental cursor sync cannot detect deletions.** Section 5.6 mandates
  forward-only cursor sync so writes stay tied to arrival rate. Section 6.1 requires
  marking comments deleted when they vanish from the platform. Forward-only sync
  never revisits older comments, so it structurally cannot see a deletion. Two
  Full-depth sections reach incompatible conclusions. Webhooks would resolve it, and
  webhooks are explicitly descoped.
- **The subscription model has nowhere to live.** Section 0 picks tier 2, opt-in
  per-post watching, as the central scope decision. Nothing in the schema records
  that a post is subscribed, and `CommentSyncState` has no index on the field the
  scheduler claims against.
- **No claim lease, reaper, or dead-letter design.** A worker claims a thousand
  posts, then spends minutes on rate-limited platform calls. Heroku cycles dynos at
  least daily. Those rows stay claimed with no visibility timeout and no recovery
  path. This is the classic failure mode of database-as-queue, and it is the one
  thing the plan owes detail on given that it chose that pattern.
- **Two idempotency mechanisms.** There is a dedicated `IdempotencyKey` table and a
  column on `Comment` with its own unique constraint. Pick one.
- **Erasure cannot execute.** Section 6.3 nulls out the text field for a deletion
  request. That column is non-nullable. Retention is configurable per workspace but
  `Workspace` has no retention field.
- **The post id resolution problem is unaddressed.** The research file flags that
  Blotato's post creation returns a tracking id, not the platform-native id. Comment
  retrieval needs the native id. Nothing says how or when that gets resolved.
- **One table for two entities.** Inbound audience comments are immutable and have no
  lifecycle. Outbound replies are mutable with retry counts, backoff, and idempotency.
  Merging them means the ingestion hot path shares indexes with the job runner's
  polling query, and every inbound row carries six unused columns. This is defensible
  for a take-home and matches the real API's shape, but the plan presents it as a
  virtue without naming the tradeoff.

Two omissions matter more than most of what is present. Cursor pagination design for
the list endpoint is one line reading `cursor?`, yet stable keyset pagination under
concurrent inserts is the actual meat of the core ask. And nothing decides what a
client sees when it requests comments for a post the store has not synced yet, which
given the platform's own no-backfill constraint is the most consequential API
semantic in the brief.

Minor API gap: the list endpoint requires `postId` but the schema carries both an
internal id and a platform post id. Which one the parameter accepts is unspecified.

## What is genuinely good

- The capability registry plus provider interface is the correct answer to the
  multi-platform requirement and is the highest-value thing to actually build.
- Returning 201 queued rather than calling the platform synchronously, justified by
  a concrete platform request timeout, is a strong decision well argued.
- Unconstrained threading depth in the schema with per-platform enforcement
  correctly identifies and fixes a flaw in the real API rather than copying it.
- Deriving actor type from the credential and never from the request body is the
  right security instinct.
- Persisting generated replies the instant the provider call returns is a correct
  and non-obvious durability point.
- The research writeup on the live API is solid groundwork that most candidates
  would skip.
- SHA-256 over bcrypt for high-entropy API keys is correct and correctly reasoned.

## What to do next, in order

1. **Answer the four open decision points today.** Suggested: job runner real,
   in-memory repositories with the Prisma schema as artifact, two providers plus one
   registered-unsupported, idempotency real and delete documented.
2. **Swap in the R1-R3 inputs and re-derive.** Mean of ~25 with 500 relabeled as a
   per-post cap, 25% reply-eligible, backoff-based polling. Every downstream figure
   follows mechanically from the revised sizing chain table.
3. **Cut the Fermi and capacity sections to about a third.** Keep the mean/burst/cap
   table, the per-account budget feasibility check, and the LLM cost table. Delete
   section 5.6 and the per-service capacity derivations, since every instance count
   becomes a redundancy decision. Add the mean column and drop the invalid percentile
   Sum row.
4. **Adopt the budget framing from R4 as the scheduler's design.** This is the
   highest-value structural change available, it makes the design feasible against
   platform limits rather than merely cheaper, and it forces the one genuinely hard
   question the plan has not asked: how budget-aware claiming works under
   database-as-queue when the quota is a shared pool.
5. **Add a quota dimension to the capability registry, and check each platform against
   it before promising support.** YouTube is infeasible at this scale on default
   quota at any poll interval, which means onboarding a platform has a business-process
   gate, not only an implementation one. This is a concrete extension of the registry
   idea the plan already got right, and it is the kind of finding that reads as real
   operating experience.
6. **Fix the workspace scoping on the three unique constraints and add the workspace
   column to `Comment`** before the schema gets copied into code.
7. **Write the pagination contract and the cold-store read semantics.** Two
   paragraphs, and they cover the literal task requirement more directly than
   anything currently marked Full.
