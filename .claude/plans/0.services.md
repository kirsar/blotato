## Roster

Six logical roles, collapsing into five physical services — Scheduler and Comment
Retriever merge, for reasons given under Scheduler-Retriever below.

| Logical role | One line | Physical service |
|---|---|---|
| API Service | serves our clients from our DB; turn automation on/off per composition | **API service** |
| Scheduler | picks which posts are due for a comment refresh | **Scheduler-Retriever** |
| Comment Retriever | fetches those comments from the platform | **Scheduler-Retriever** |
| Content Generator | decides which comments deserve a reply, and writes it | **Content Generator** |
| Reply Publisher | delivers approved replies to the platform | **Reply Publisher** |
| Webhook Delivery | notifies *our customers* that a comment or reply landed | **Webhook Delivery** ⛔ out of scope as of now |

Each is broken down below as **Logic → Load → Capacity → Host → Scale → When/How to
scale**. Five are built; the sixth is specified so the shape is legible and the seam is
visible, but no worker exists.

- **API service** — serves *our own* clients from *our own* database (list stored
  comments, accept reply requests); no platform calls or polling here (that's
  Scheduler-Retriever).
  - **Logic**: a request handler, not a loop — the only service here with no queue.
    Reads are served straight from Postgres, so `GET /comments` never triggers a
    platform fetch; a post whose comments haven't synced yet returns what we have, not
    a 202. Writes are accepted and queued, never executed inline: `POST /comments`
    validates against the platform's capability row, writes a `Comment` at `QUEUED`,
    and returns `201` immediately — Reply Publisher does the delivery. It also owns
    the automation surface that sets `Composition.commentAutomationLevel` (or a
    `PostSchedule` override), materializes the schedule row and seeds `nextPollAfter`,
    which is what puts a post into the Scheduler-Retriever loop at all.
  - **Load**: ~3.9 posts/sec is the write-side anchor. **Client read volume is not
    estimated anywhere in this plan** — a real gap, since reads are argued to dominate
    ([0.capacity-planning.md](./0.capacity-planning.md), Statistical inputs) and this is the service that serves them. Sizing below is
    against writes and holds only while reads stay within the same order of magnitude.
  - **Capacity**: single instance handles low-hundreds to low-thousands req/sec for
    indexed CRUD (a range, depends on sizing/pool tuning) — ~4/sec isn't close to it.
  - **Host**: its own process role — trivially separate from the three loops (below),
    same shape-based reasoning as Scheduler-Retriever's, just the simple direction of
    it.
  - **Scale**: 3+ replicas from day one — not for throughput (4/sec doesn't need it),
    but for real redundancy: 2 is only the bare minimum to survive one
    failure, leaving zero spare capacity until it's replaced; 3+ survives a failure
    with headroom left over, the standard production-grade default. No scaling needed
    beyond that now, and no decide-vs-execute duality to split further here — replica
    count is the only lever.
  - **When to scale**: observed latency/CPU saturation on existing replicas, or
    proactively as the ceiling nears — not the 4/sec figure itself.
  - **How to scale**: add replicas behind a reverse proxy — Heroku's router already
    fills that role (load-balances across replicas, terminates TLS, resolves DNS for
    the app's domain), nothing built ourselves. Stateless (state lives in Postgres) —
    any replica serves any request, no coordination needed. For global growth: a
    latency floor no amount of single-region scaling fixes — solved by a custom DNS
    resolver (GeoDNS/GSLB, or anycast), routing each client to its nearest region;
    that region's reverse proxy then load-balances locally as above. Not needed yet.
- **Scheduler-Retriever service** — merges the Scheduler and Comment Retriever roles.
  They stay one worker because splitting them would mean a second claim cycle for no
  gain: once dispatch is concurrent, "decide what's due" and "go fetch it" have the
  same rate, the same account-budget constraint, and the same failure modes.
  - **Logic** — one loop, several outcomes, no separate jobs:
    1. **Claim** posts whose `nextPollAfter` has passed, `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`,
       batch sized to top up one instance's dispatcher (~100-200).
    2. **Retire or fetch.** The same scan that finds due posts also finds ones past the
       polling window or marked for deletion, and writes a terminal status instead of
       re-queueing. **Two distinct age thresholds live here**: stop polling at ~14 days,
       delete data at 45 ([0.capacity-planning.md](./0.capacity-planning.md) block 2) —
       conflating them was the single largest source of wasted quota.
    3. **Fetch incrementally** from the cursor in `CommentSyncState`, never a full
       re-read: at ~430 polls/sec × ~50 comments that would be ~21,500 upserts/sec
       against ~195/sec with cursors.
    4. **Reschedule adaptively.** Due time is computed *per post* from observed comment
       velocity, not read from a global constant — target-yield while active, geometric
       backoff on consecutive empty polls, reset from velocity rather than snapping to
       the floor. Intervals are **jittered**, which matters specifically here because a
       scheduling tool publishes many posts in the same minute and a deterministic
       schedule would keep them phase-aligned for their whole lifetime.

    [0.capacity-planning.md](./0.capacity-planning.md) block 2 is the specification for
    steps 2 and 4; this section only consumes it.
  - **Load**: **~430/sec** retrieval — post arrival rate × ~110 polls per post
    lifetime, derived in [0.capacity-planning.md](./0.capacity-planning.md) block 2 along with the polling mechanisms
    that produce it. A flat 1000s poll across the full 45-day retention window would be
    ~15,000/sec — ~22x over Meta's effective ceiling, and rejected rather than
    conservative. Adopting the 14-day polling window from that same section takes this
    to ~185/sec. Levers if the ceiling binds are in
    [0.capacity-planning.md](./0.capacity-planning.md) §5 — webhooks buy quota, batching
    buys only latency.
  - **Capacity**: ~430 fetches/sec, each needing an external call, JSON parsing, and a
    Postgres upsert. **Connections**: a call is in flight ~100-300ms, so steady-state
    concurrency is ~130 (430/sec × 300ms) — well inside one instance's ~1,000
    sustainable connections, so **capacity needs one instance, not five**. Still
    dispatched via a bounded-concurrency pool nesting a per-account rate limiter
    (§3.3), because the constraint that matters is the platform quota, not our
    connection count. **Claim batch size**: the claim `LIMIT` should top up one
    instance's dispatcher, so ~100-200; cheap regardless (~5-20ms/batch, DB-only). **CPU**: parsing ~50 comments per
    response on average, up to the 500 cap — secondary to external-call latency.
    **Postgres upserts**: comments must still write incrementally (cursor-based via
    `CommentSyncState`, [5.storage.md](./5.storage.md)); a full re-fetch-and-upsert
    every poll would mean ~430/sec × ~50 ≈ 21,500 comment-row upserts/sec, versus
    ~195/sec with incremental sync — a 110x gap, and the argument for cursors.
  - **Host**: its own process — claim and fetch merged into one pipeline (no reason for
    a second claim stage once dispatch is concurrent), separate from the API service
    (loop vs. handler) and from Reply Publisher/Content Generator (each has a different
    external dependency and rate-limit profile worth isolating).
  - **Scale**: **3 instances — the redundancy baseline, not a capacity figure.**
    Capacity needs one.
  - **When to scale**: **the binding signal is the platform quota, not our
    infrastructure.** Adding instances does not help there — it is the one saturation
    signal that more workers make worse, by spending the shared pool faster (Capacity
    planning, block 2). Our own limits, concurrent connections and Postgres upsert
    latency, sit at roughly a tenth of their ceilings and are unlikely to bind first.
  - **How to scale**: add instances — `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` keeps claiming
    coordination-free at any count — but only up to the platform ceiling. Past that,
    fix demand before supply. Note that adaptive interval and empty-poll backoff are
    **already spent**: they are baseline in [0.capacity-planning.md](./0.capacity-planning.md) block 2 and are assumed
    by the ~430/sec above, so they are not contingency. What remains in reserve is in
    [0.capacity-planning.md](./0.capacity-planning.md) §5 — webhooks, which are the only lever that buys quota, the
    14-day polling window, and a quota-increase request. Pooling multiple app
    credentials is **not** a lever; see that section for why.
- **Content Generator service** — decides which inbound comments warrant a reply and
  generates the reply text. Bound by the external AI provider's rate and cost limits,
  not our infra.
  - **Logic** — a filter in front of an expensive call:
    1. **Claim** inbound comments with no reply yet, via
       `@@index([parentCommentId, isAuthor])` — the alternative is an unindexed
       `NOT EXISTS` self-join every cycle.
    2. **Filter before spending anything.** ~75% of comments are low-signal, and
       dropping them needs length thresholds, emoji-only detection, and a small
       classifier — not a frontier model. This runs *before* the provider call, which
       makes it the highest-leverage cost decision in the pipeline (§4.5).
    3. **Group the survivors by `(userId, accountId)`** and generate one call per group,
       with the platform's `maxCommentLength` applied as a *prompt constraint*, not
       post-hoc validation — TikTok's 150-char cap sits ~1.7x under the default reply
       budget, so validating after would mean paying for replies that get rejected. At
       steady-state volumes a group holds ~1.07 comments, so this is ordinarily one
       reply per call; it fills only under the bursts where the provider ceiling
       actually binds. The partition rules and size caps are
       [4.agentic-integration.md](./4.agentic-integration.md)'s.
    4. **Persist immediately** at `status=QUEUED`, the moment the call returns (§5.1).
       The provider call is the expensive, non-repeatable part; losing the result after
       a successful call means paying again, not retrying a cheap write. `DRAFT` is out
       of scope end to end (§1.2), so there is no promotion step and no client-facing
       promotion endpoint — the row enters the Reply Publisher's queue directly.
  - **Load**: **~49 reply generations/sec** — system-wide arrival ~195/sec × 25%
    reply-eligible ([0.capacity-planning.md](./0.capacity-planning.md) §B). Tenant
    isolation holds by construction, since an account belongs to exactly one tenant
    (§7.0), and each reply is written to `Comment` at
    `QUEUED` status as soon as the call returns (§5.1), with no buffering in memory.
  - **Batching does not pay at *average* volumes.** Eligible replies arrive at
    ~4.9×10^-4/sec/account, so even a 300s batching window holds ~0.15 replies per
    account: only ~14% of accounts have anything at all, and those that do average
    **~1.07 replies per batch**. Widening the key past the account doesn't rescue it —
    the ~400-token brand-voice preamble is per account, so an agency's two brands can't
    share one. **So in steady state: roughly one reply per call, ~49 calls/sec.**
    The grouping still exists, because arrival is heavily skewed and a viral post
    concentrates hundreds of comments on one account in the minutes when the provider
    ceiling binds — a batch that doesn't fill is a batch of one, so it costs nothing to
    keep ([4.agentic-integration.md](./4.agentic-integration.md)).
  - **Capacity**: without batching, latency is ~1s fixed overhead + ~65 output tokens
    ÷ ~50 tokens/sec ≈ **~2.3s/call**. Concurrency = rate × time in flight ≈ 49 × 2.3
    ≈ **~113 in-flight calls** — inside one instance. CPU is negligible, prompt
    assembly only. Postgres load is one read to assemble and one insert per reply at
    ~49/sec, far lighter than ingestion.
  - **Cost is the binding constraint here, not concurrency, and losing batching makes
    it worse.** Unbatched, each reply pays the full ~400-token preamble: ~615 tokens
    per reply versus ~240 when amortized across 15. At ~1.27×10^8 replies/month that
    is ~78B tokens/month, roughly **$39k-156k/month** at $0.50-2/M. **Prompt caching is
    the lever that recovers most of it** — the per-user preamble is byte-identical
    across that user's calls, so caching it cuts the effective figure to ~255
    tokens/reply and the bill to roughly **$16k-65k/month**. Caching, not batching, is
    what makes the cost work at this volume.
  - **Host**: its own process — a different external dependency (AI provider vs.
    platform reads/writes) with its own rate-limit/backoff semantics, same isolation
    logic as Scheduler-Retriever and Reply Publisher.
  - **Scale**: **3 instances, redundancy baseline.** ~113 in-flight calls fit in one
    instance, so capacity needs one.
  - **When to scale**: provider 429s or token-per-minute throttling climbing, or the
    reply backlog growing faster than it drains. Cost per reply is worth watching on
    the same dashboard — it binds before concurrency does.
  - **How to scale**: add instances up to the provider's own rate ceiling. Beyond
    that, §4.5's demand levers apply, now reordered by what actually pays: prompt
    caching on the per-user preamble first, then tightening the low-signal filter
    below 25%, then pooling provider keys or orgs, then accepting latency. Batch size
    is not a steady-state lever — see Load above — but it is what absorbs a burst.
- **Reply Publisher service** — delivers approved (`QUEUED`) replies to the platform
  (the write side of what was "platform integration"; retrieval now lives in
  Scheduler-Retriever, above).
  - **Logic** — the simplest of the five, and deliberately so:
    1. **Select** the outbound `Comment` rows still at `QUEUED` on the posts this pass
       has claimed. `status` *is* the pending predicate, and reaching them by post means
       `(postId, platformCreatedAt)` narrows the scan before `status` filters it — no
       due-time column and no index on `status` anywhere.
    2. **Post** through the platform provider, dispatched via a bounded-concurrency
       pool nesting a per-account rate limiter (§3.3) — the account's write budget is
       the constraint, not our concurrency.
    3. **Record the outcome**: on success, set `Comment.status` `POSTED` with the
       returned `platformCommentId`; on a terminal failure, `FAILED` with
       `errorCode`/`errorMessage`. A retryable failure writes nothing at all — the row
       stays `QUEUED` and is retried when its post next falls due, so the adaptive poll
       interval paces retries without an attempt counter.
    4. **Writing `platformCommentId` is load-bearing beyond bookkeeping.** Our own
       reply comes back as an ordinary comment on the next poll; the tenant-scoped
       unique on `(userId, platform, platformCommentId)` makes that an idempotent
       upsert. If the post succeeds but the status write fails, the next poll ingests
       our own reply as fresh audience input and the generator may reply to itself.
  - **Load**: **~49 replies/sec** (25% reply-eligible, [0.capacity-planning.md](./0.capacity-planning.md) §B) — one
    HTTP call per reply; most platforms don't accept a batch post (Capacity planning
    §5), so this is naturally per-item.
  - **Capacity**: platform posting calls run ~100-300ms — ~49/sec × ~300ms ≈ **~15
    concurrent in-flight calls**, trivially inside one instance. CPU negligible,
    payload formatting only. Dispatched via a bounded-concurrency pool nesting a rate
    limiter per account (§3.3), same pattern as Scheduler-Retriever. Reads `QUEUED`
    rows and writes back `POSTED`/`FAILED` — single-row updates, negligible against
    ingestion.
  - **The real constraint is Instagram's write cap, not our capacity**: 60 writes/hour
    per user on `/media/comments`. At ~49/sec across ~10^5 accounts that is
    ~1.8/hour/account, ~3% of the cap ([0.capacity-planning.md](./0.capacity-planning.md) block 4). The old 1,448/sec
    figure would have sat at ~52/hour, or 87% of a hard cap assuming perfectly even
    distribution across accounts — which never holds, so it would have failed in
    practice well before that.
  - **Host independently.** (1) Load is decorrelated from Scheduler-Retriever's —
    posting only happens for the fraction of comments Content Generator/guardrails
    approve, after a processing lag, not 1:1 with retrieval volume. (2) Calls block on
    platform latency and each platform's own rate limits — colocated with anything
    else, a slow platform call would starve unrelated work. (3) Holds OAuth
    credentials for every connected account — isolating it narrows blast radius
    (§7.3).
  - **Scale**: **3 instances**, the standard redundancy baseline — capacity alone
    needs well under one instance.
  - **When to scale**: observed posting latency/backoff creeping up, or platform 429s
    climbing.
  - **How to scale**: add instances up to each platform's own rate ceiling (§3.3) —
    beyond that, same external-limit conclusion as Scheduler-Retriever/Content
    Generator: pool app credentials where the platform allows it, accept latency
    (posting isn't hard real-time either, §0).
- **Webhook Delivery service — ⛔ OUT OF SCOPE AS OF NOW.** Notifies *our customers*
  when a comment arrives or a reply posts, so integrators aren't forced to poll our API
  the way this design refuses to poll the platforms. Sized here because the shape is
  settled and cheap to state; **not built** — schema and route only
  (current-api-overview.md #5).
  - **Logic**: a pure consumer of a `WebhookDelivery` queue. It never reads `Comment`.
    The *producers* emit: Scheduler-Retriever inserts delivery rows in the same
    micro-batched transaction as the comment upsert, Reply Publisher does the same when
    a reply reaches `POSTED`. Writing in the producer's transaction makes emission
    exactly-once by construction — no watermark, no "which comments were already
    notified" query. Fan-out (one event → N subscriptions) is materialized at emit
    time from a cached, tenant-scoped subscription map, so the emit is a lookup that
    is usually a no-op. The worker then claims `QUEUED` rows, POSTs the frozen payload
    with an HMAC signature, and updates status/attempt/backoff.
  - **Load**: bounded above by ~195 comment events/sec + ~49 reply events/sec ≈ **~244
    deliveries/sec** at full subscription uptake, and far lower in practice since most
    users will have no subscription. Multiplies by subscriptions per user,
    typically 0 or 1.
  - **Capacity**: customer endpoints are slower and less predictable than platform APIs
    — assume ~500ms-2s with a hard timeout. At ~244/sec × ~1s ≈ **~244 concurrent
    in-flight calls**, still inside one instance. Postgres load is one claim query plus
    single-row status updates, negligible against ingestion.
  - **Host independently**, and this is the load-bearing reason rather than a
    stylistic one: it blocks on **customer** endpoint health, not platform rate limits.
    Colocated with Reply Publisher, one hanging subscriber would starve reply posting.
    Different failure profile, different isolation boundary (§1.4's standing logic).
  - **Scale**: **3 instances** if built, redundancy baseline — capacity needs one.
  - **When to scale**: delivery backlog growing faster than it drains, or p99 delivery
    latency tracking one slow subscriber rather than the fleet.
  - **How to scale**: add instances. Unlike every other worker here there is **no
    external ceiling** — subscriber endpoints are independent, so this is the one
    service that genuinely scales by adding capacity. The limits that do bite are
    per-subscriber: short timeouts, and auto-disable after N consecutive failures.
  - **Why it isn't built**: the delivery mechanics are `ReplyJobRunner`'s exact shape,
    but the URL is attacker-controlled, and SSRF protection is not deferrable — a
    delivery worker without address filtering is a credential-exfiltration path
    (current-api-overview.md, "Why #5 is a stub"). All-of-it or none-of-it; the
    take-home budget doesn't cover all of it.
